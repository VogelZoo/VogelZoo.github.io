Methodology & limitations

This is a game inspired by pseudoisochromatic plate tests like the Ishihara test, built from published color-vision-deficiency (CVD) science. It is not a validated clinical instrument.

How each plate is generated

  • Every round targets one cone confusion axis: protan (L-cone), deutan (M-cone), or, in Full Spectrum mode, the rare tritan (S-cone) axis.
  • A background color is chosen at random. The game then searches hundreds of candidate figure colors and keeps the one that becomes nearly identical to the background when both are run through a dichromacy simulation — i.e. a real confusion pair for that axis, not just a random hue shift.
  • The simulation uses the physiologically-based transform of Machado, Oliveira & Fernandes (2009), applied in linear-light RGB. Milder "anomalous trichromat" levels are approximated by linearly blending toward the full-dichromat transform, following the interpolation approach described by Flatla & Gutwin (2012).
  • Color closeness is measured with CIE L*a*b* ΔE (CIE76). Candidate figure colors are only accepted if they stay at least ΔE 14 from the background in normal, untransformed vision; among those, the one with the smallest simulated distance is kept. This keeps every plate legible to typical color vision while still collapsing under the CVD simulation.
  • "I don't see a shape" is scored the same as any other miss. It exists so a genuine non-detection isn't forced into a guess among the visible shape options, which would otherwise occasionally land on the right answer by chance and understate a real difficulty.
  • Per-dot color noise is applied in L*a*b* space (not raw hue/saturation), which is what keeps each "color family" varied without breaking the underlying confusion pairing — the same design idea used in real hidden-digit plates.

How the sequence is structured

Each tested axis (deutan/protan, plus tritan in Full Spectrum mode) is presented at five simulated severities — from full dichromacy (1.0) down to mild anomalous trichromacy (0.3) — twice each, in random order. Dot size, spacing, and jitter are held fixed for every plate, so a miss traces back to the axis and severity being tested rather than the plate simply getting visually busier.

How results are compiled

Nothing is revealed as correct or incorrect while you're working through the plates. At the end, responses are grouped by axis and scored for accuracy, and the severities of any missed plates on that axis are reported. This is meant to loosely mirror how graded clinical batteries separate strong dichromats from mild anomalous trichromats — it is not a scored clinical instrument, and the categories shown ("no pattern detected," "some inconsistency," "difficulty pattern") are informal accuracy bands, not diagnostic classifications.

Real-world prevalence used for axis weighting

Red-green deficiency accounts for the large majority of congenital CVD, appearing in roughly 8% of men and 0.5% of women of Northern European descent, lower in other populations. Deutan defects (~6% of men) are markedly more common than protan defects (~1–2% of men). Tritan (blue-yellow) deficiency is rare and, unlike red-green CVD, affects males and females roughly equally.

Honest limitations

  • This runs on your uncalibrated screen, under your ambient lighting — real plate tests are printed and standardized against a specific illuminant. Neither this nor any on-screen test replaces one.
  • It has not been validated against diagnosed patients. It cannot diagnose color vision deficiency, only give you a playful, science-grounded sense of confusion-axis difficulty.
  • If you suspect a color vision deficiency, an optometrist or ophthalmologist can test you properly with calibrated plates or an anomaloscope.
